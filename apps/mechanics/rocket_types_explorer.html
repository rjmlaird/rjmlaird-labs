Propellant, payload and energy

Bars are on a log scale. Chemical energy uses rough values per kilogram of propellant mix (RP-1/LOX 10.3 MJ, LH2/LOX 13.4, methane/LOX 10.0, solid 6.5, hypergolic 6.0). Payload energy is the kinetic plus potential energy of the published LEO payload in a 200 km orbit (about 32 MJ/kg). Most of a rocket’s mass is propellant, and only a small share of the chemical energy ends up in the payload; the rest leaves as exhaust or is lost to gravity and drag. Hydrogen engines give more Δv per kilogram, but hydrogen is bulky and the engines are complex; dense fuels give more thrust per tank.

The rocket equation

Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln(m₀ / mf)

The Δv grows with Isp and with the logarithm of the mass ratio (wet mass m₀ over final mass mf). Dropping empty stages keeps mf small, which is why every rocket here is staged.

Orbital and escape velocity

An object launched sideways from low altitude, at different fractions of circular orbital speed (about 7.8 km/s). Below 1.0 it falls back; at 1.0 it circles; between 1.0 and √2 it follows an ellipse; at √2 (about 11.2 km/s from the surface) it escapes. This ignores the atmosphere, and real rockets reach these speeds gradually over several minutes.
