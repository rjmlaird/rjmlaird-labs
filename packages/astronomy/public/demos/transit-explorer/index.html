Exoplanet transit and light-curve explorer

A planet passes in front of its star, and the star's brightness dips. Change the planet's size, its distance from the star and how centrally it crosses, and watch the light curve respond. Add noise to see why small planets are hard to find.

Presets:

The animation is the view from Earth: the planet crosses the star's face. The planet is drawn at true scale relative to the star, but never smaller than a couple of pixels, so tiny planets look larger than they are. Impact parameter b is how far from the star's centre the planet's path passes, in stellar radii: 0 is straight across the middle, and above 1 + Rp/R★ the planet misses the star. The planet's size is in Earth radii (R⊕).

Derived quantities

The transit method. When a planet crosses its star, it blocks a little of the starlight. The fraction blocked is roughly the ratio of the two disc areas, (Rp / R★)². Astronomers measure the star's brightness again and again (photometry) and look for these small, regular dips.

  • Planet size sets the depth. An Earth crossing the Sun blocks about 84 parts per million; a Jupiter blocks about 1%.
  • Orbital distance sets the period (P² ∝ a³ / M★). Closer planets transit more often, and more of them happen to line up with us: the chance is about R★ / a.
  • Impact parameter sets how long the transit lasts and how its shape looks. A central transit has a long, flat bottom; a near-grazing one is short and V-shaped.
  • Limb darkening. A star is dimmer at its edge than in the middle. This rounds the bottom of the dip, so the curve's shape carries information about the star and the path.
  • Noise. Real measurements scatter. The transit signal-to-noise ratio grows with depth and with the number of measurements inside the transit, which is why detections usually need several transits and a very steady telescope: space telescopes measure brightness to tens of ppm.
  • Simplified model. A circular orbit, a dark planet, no starspots, and only the transit (no eclipse of the planet behind the star). Limb-darkening values and stellar sizes are illustrative.
